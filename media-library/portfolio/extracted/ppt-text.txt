BIO:
Labani Jangi: is a research scholar, cultural practitioner, and artist from Nadia, West Bengal, currently pursuing her PhD at the Centre for Studies in Social Sciences, Calcutta. Her doctoral work, on the life world of Bengali migrant labour, extends her M.Phil. research on labour migration from Nadia and Murshidabad. Her creative practice intersects with land, politics, and community, as seen in her international solo exhibition “BIBIR DARGA” in Chattogram, Bangladesh. Labani’s work has been showcased widely, including at IIT Gandhinagar’s Comics Conclave 2.0, the Anti-Imperialist Poster Exhibition, and the Museum of Conflict, Raipur. 
Since 2020, Labani has been freelancing for the People‟s Archive of Rural India (PARI), contributing to rural journalism and archiving. Her writings, illustrations, and book covers have been published internationally, including in Bangla Journal (Canada), Ground Xero (India), Suddeutsche Zeitung (Germany), and Samhati Publication (Dhaka, Bangladesh). Her notable contributions include cover art for Sultana's Sisters: Genre, Gender, and Genealogy in South Asian Muslim Women's Fiction (Routledge, 2021) and cover art for Making Death and Life in Palestine: Social Reproduction in Settler Colonialism (500 Milis, 2025) edited by Tithi Bhattacharya and Susan Ferguson.

ARTIST STATEMENT
 
I want to tell stories, draw emotions, passion for life and find language to express what I see in my everyday surroundings. Through my art, I explore a layered tapestry of inquiry—observing the currents of resistance and identity formation beyond protected ignorance as they unfold in the world around me. As a self-taught artist and researcher, my work is deeply rooted in lived experiences, reflecting the fractures and solidarities of a world where subaltern voices are systematically invisibilized or romanticized. I draw inspiration from the daily realities of being a Muslim woman in a society where hatred is normalized, and state-sponsored violence permeates everyday life.

The JARI GAAN tradition serves as an inspiration to my work—a centuries-old form that intertwines sorrow, resistance, and communal memory. This tradition embodies resilience against historical and ongoing oppressions, acting not merely as a subject but as a vital language connecting me to a lineage of survival and expression. For over a decade, I have documented and reimagined the Bengali Migrant Labourers, SUNNI MUSLIM MUHARRAM and JARI GAAN traditions through illustration, video documenting, and other mediums. These efforts aim to resist cultural homogenization and existential threats to pluralism. Art becomes a site of dialogue—a bridge fostering connections amidst fragmentation. It transforms personal narratives into collective voices, inviting reflection on the intersecting threads of identity, culture, and resistance. By weaving memory and imagination, I aspire to create spaces where these stories are not only remembered but reasserted as vital elements of our shared human experience.

The urgency for a utopia drives my imagination; envisioning a new society becomes imperative in response to the violence that surrounds me. My drawings capture everyday encounters—people, landscapes, emotions—tracing the love and passion for life that persists despite chaos. The act of image-making attempts to address the question of representation; it becomes an essential practice in the politics of dissemination across various platforms—from street art to journals, social media to local public spaces. Through my work, I explore the collective experience of subaltern communities, documenting their struggles against systemic violence while celebrating their resilience. Each piece is imbued with traces of love—for life, for community, for stories that deserve to be told. In this context, art is not just an individual pursuit; it is an act of solidarity with those who endure oppression.

I am also a member of the Panjeri Artists’ Union, JINN (an independent publication), and Khandera Art Space. These collective engagements shape the way I think and work, allowing us to move beyond the capitalist alienations and exploitative nature of artistic practice. My artistic journey is an ongoing exploration of identity amidst adversity. It seeks to illuminate the complexities of existence as a Muslim woman navigating a world rife with hatred while nurturing dreams of hope and change. Through my art, I strive to contribute to a broader narrative that champions diversity, resilience, and the transformative power of imagination in the face of violence.

Rural Realities: 
Documenting Lives Through Journalism and Art
As a 2020 Fellow of the People’s Archive of Rural India (PARI), I started understanding the complexities of rural life. Since then, I have continued freelancing for PARI, contributing to their essential mission of rural journalism and archiving. Through our publications in 14 languages, I have had the privilege of documenting and amplifying the lives, struggles, and resilience of communities often marginalized in mainstream narratives.

This journey has been transformative, offering me an intimate view of the socio-political landscapes of rural India. Meeting artists, activists, journalists, and poets from diverse regions has deepened my understanding of the cultural and political dynamics shaping these communities. These encounters inform my artistic practice, which seeks to reflect the lived realities of rural existence.

My work with PARI focuses on capturing stories that bring attention to both the systemic challenges and the enduring spirit of rural life. By integrating these narratives into my art, I aim to create visual expressions that resonate with the complexities of these experiences. My illustrations become a medium through which I contribute to preserving and sharing the cultural and social reality of rural India.

Currently, I am pursuing a PhD at the Centre for Studies in Social Sciences, Kolkata, focusing on labour migrations. This research complements my work with PARI, allowing me to approach rural stories with a critical lens shaped by both academic rigor and on-ground engagement.

Through this ongoing journey, I remain committed to documenting the rich tapestry of rural India, bridging journalism and art to create spaces for dialogue and understanding.

BIBIR DARGA

A solo show at
 CHITRABHASHA Art Gallery, Chattogram, Bangladesh; December 2022
The name of my birthplace is Battala—a name that resonates with the quiet weight of loss. Today, Battala bears the scars of erasure: its banyan trees, once guardians of memory and community, have been cut down in the name of national highway expansion. This is not mere 'development' but an act of violence—a silencing of histories, landscapes, and lives deeply intertwined with the land. What remains is desolation, where swirling winds stir memories that evade words. In response, I turn to colours and brushes, capturing fragments of dreams, rivers, and skies that linger between memory and imagination.

The first river I encountered as a child was known locally as the KHORE River, though maps label it as JALANGI. The dissonance of names reflects its unmarked existence in official records. Over time, KHORE River has lost its navigability, its waters now heavy with sediment and loss. Yet, it continues to flow in my memories, twisting and turning into dreams that demand expression. With my brush, I attempt to archive those dreams, not as static records but as living testaments to the fragility of place and identity.

NOTES: 

SLIDE 46
Nearby, a SHIMUL (silk cotton tree) stood as a witness to life and death, a site where funeral prayers brought the village together. Local lore, shared by my friend Bhaswati, holds that pairs of silk cotton trees mark pathways to heaven. These trees, along with the banyans of Battala, shaped the emotional and experiential landscape of my childhood. But this sacred canopy has been systematically destroyed—BIBIR DARGA (shrine), once shaded by banyan trees, was cleared in the name of ideological purity, its clay horse offerings labelled as SHIRK (one of the gravest sins in Islam). In its place now stands a sanitized courtyard, stripped of its layered histories.

This act of destruction is not unique to Battala; it reflects a broader pattern of erasing the complexities of memory and resistance. My work does not attempt to resolve this violence but to confront it, fragment by fragment. BIBIR DARGA is an assemblage of these fragments—a mosaic of memories, dreams, and voids that resist the linearity of narrative. The works are steeped in incompleteness, embodying the tension between my longing to preserve and the impossibility of doing so.

In a world where political discourse often aligns with destruction, my paintings emerge as a softer yet equally potent language of protest. Each brushstroke captures the dissonance between loss and survival, between despair and hope. Through "BIBIR DARGA," I navigate these contradictions, crafting spaces for contemplation and resistance. This exhibition marks my first solo presentation at CHITRABHASHA Gallery in Chattogram, Bangladesh. It is both deeply personal and inherently political, an offering to the collective memory of landscapes and lives that have been erased or forgotten. "BIBIR DARGA" invites viewers to engage with these fragments—not to seek resolution but to share in the act of bearing witness. May this collection stand as a quiet rebellion against erasure, a testament to the enduring power of memory, and a call to reimagine what can yet be reclaimed.

Collective Praxis: Art, Labor, and Resistance
In contemporary art, collaboration often remains uncredited, with collective efforts absorbed into individual cultural capital—easily converted into economic capital. This process invisibilizes tacit knowledge, artistic/material labor, and instead champions immaterial/intellectual labor.

To counter this, we organize under three initiatives:

“JINN” an independent publication

Panjeri Artists’ Union focuses on the unionization of artistic labor, advocating for the recognition of artistic/material labor and contributing artistic production to anti-caste struggles in Bengal. 

Khandera Community Artspace fosters collective artistic practices and critical discourse, particularly resisting state-led land acquisitions like the National Thermal Power Plant, Dadri, and Special Economic Zones (SEZs), which disproportionately displace marginalized communities. 

Both spaces work to reclaim authorship, foreground artistic labor, and build solidarities against systemic dispossession and social hierarchies.

SLIDE 67

‘Panjeri’  a name associated with navigation, is a tool that gives the boatman a vision to transcend the obscure veil of the mighty river. It protects him from going astray in the enormity of the oceanic haziness. 
By climbing a vessel’s mast and floating into the nocturnal space, Panjeri becomes a sailor's confidant. It reveals his surroundings, weather conditions and the enigmatic bars and beyond. The device helps him to sail across riverine Bengal's water-routes and maritime pathways. What resonance, however, does this singular word have on our collective consciousness? 
To quote the poet Farrukh Ahmed,
“রাত পোহাবার কত দেরি পাঞ্জেরি? 
দীঘল রাতের শ্রান্তসফর শেষে 
কোন দরিয়ার কালো দিগন্তে আমরা পড়েছি এসে?” 
"How much longer does the night linger, Panjeri?
At the end of the prolonged journey through the weary night,
Which mighty river’s dim horizon have we reached?"
 
Within the contours of the arc of time in which we live, society as a whole seems to be enveloped in the liminality of turbulent identities, a profound epistemic confusion, and the unfathomable inconspicuousness of the dominant ideology. A palpable anxiety resonates, stemming from the encroaching neo-liberal trend of self-centeredness, unbridled hedonism, and indulgence in falsehoods of oppressive structures. 

On this murky precipice, we yearn for connection, a bond that can stem the tide of separation that threatens to scatter us among the rubble of our times. As navigators crossing the stormy seas of our socio-political existence, we ask Panjeri, our collective beacon, to light our path. And there are times when we ourselves must transform into Panjeris, navigating our own paths as we seek direction amid the constant ebb and flow of contemporary existence.
